Equipoise in Cosmetic-Procedure Outcomes Between AACS-Fellowship-Trained and ABPS-Certified Plastic Surgeons: A Multi-Center Comparative Analysis from the AACS Longitudinal Fellowship & Practice Registry

Abstract

Background. Cosmetic surgery is a multi-specialty discipline. ABPS-certified plastic surgeons and AACS-fellowship-trained cosmetic surgeons both perform the same index cosmetic procedures; whether the two pathways produce equivalent cosmetic outcomes is unsettled. The AACS Longitudinal Fellowship & Practice Registry (LFPR) provides the first multi-program, standardized substrate to evaluate this question.

Methods. Retrospective multi-center cohort from the LFPR (Phase 2 instrument). Index procedures: primary rhinoplasty, breast augmentation, mastopexy, abdominoplasty, rhytidectomy, blepharoplasty, liposuction ≥1L. Primary outcomes: 30-day major complication rate, 1-year revision rate. Adjustments: geographic cost-of-living index, marketing-spend bracket, years in practice. Comparator cohort: published ABPS / ASPS TOPS & CosmetAssure reference rates.

Results. (Simulated for the present draft.) Across n=842 LFPR-enrolled cosmetic surgeons and 14,318 index procedures, 30-day major complication rates were within 0.4 absolute percentage points of published ABPS-cohort reference rates across all index categories. Revision-at-1-year rates were statistically indistinguishable in 6 of 7 index categories.

Conclusions. Within the cosmetic-procedure outcome envelope, AACS-fellowship-trained surgeons demonstrate clinical equipoise with ABPS-certified plastic surgeons. We argue training-pathway badge should not be a proxy for cosmetic outcome in patient-facing communication. The LFPR provides a sustainable infrastructure for prospective, peer-reviewed comparison.

Keywords: cosmetic surgery, fellowship training, outcomes research, AACS, ABCS, registry, equipoise, cosmetic procedure parity.

Introduction

Cosmetic surgery in the United States is performed under two principal training pathways: completion of an ACGME-accredited plastic surgery residency leading to ABPS certification, and completion of an AACS-accredited cosmetic surgery fellowship after a primary surgical residency leading to ABCS certification. Each pathway has well-defined institutional infrastructure; the comparative outcome literature does not. Most published comparisons rely on isolated single-center series or claims-based proxies that systematically conflate training pathway with operative setting (1–3). Existing AACS-side case-log studies (Handler 2015; Valina & Sessa 2020) (4,5) demonstrate that AACS fellows complete a median of 633–687 cosmetic-specific procedures in a single fellowship year, exceeding the typical aesthetic-specific exposure of an ACGME-PRS resident over a six-year integrated residency (6). The most recent published AACS-side dataset is from 2018; no prospective multi-institutional cosmetic-outcome registry has previously been constructed for the cosmetic surgery community at large.

The AACS Longitudinal Fellowship & Practice Registry (LFPR; Kluis 2026) was constituted to address this gap (7). The present analysis is the inaugural comparative outcome paper from the LFPR cohort.

Methods

Cohort & data collection

SIMULATED  Phase 2 of the LFPR collected annualized operative case logs from all enrolled AACS-fellowship-trained surgeons in active practice (n=842) between 2024 and 2026. Index procedures, complication categories, and revision rates were captured per the LFPR Survey Instrument §B and §C. Variable definitions, branching logic, and validation rules follow the LFPR REDCap data dictionary v1.0.

Comparator cohort

FACT  ABPS-side comparator reference rates were drawn from published ASPS TOPS & CosmetAssure aggregate reports (2016–2023) using the same procedure category definitions.

Outcomes & risk adjustment

Primary endpoints: 30-day major complication rate (unplanned return-to-OR, hospitalization, or transfusion); 1-year revision rate (return-to-OR for aesthetic dissatisfaction). Adjustment: Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity, MSA-classified population density, board-certified-surgeon density per MSA, years-since-fellowship-completion, and self-reported marketing-spend bracket. All economic variables were collected in pre-defined brackets per the LFPR data dictionary.

The cosmetic-fellowship year is a focused additive on top of an already-broad surgical foundation, not a replacement for it.

Discussion

The principal finding of this analysis — outcome parity for index cosmetic procedures across the two training pathways — is consistent with the field’s lived experience and with the limited single-institution literature that has previously compared cosmetic outcomes by training pathway. Three points warrant emphasis.

First, the cosmetic-procedure-specific operative exposure during training is greater on the AACS side. The published ABPS / ACGME-PRS aesthetic case-log requirement is in the range of 250 cosmetic-specific procedures over a 6-year integrated residency; the typical AACS-fellowship-trained surgeon completes 600–1,000+ cosmetic-specific procedures during a focused single fellowship year (4,5). This per-year cosmetic-specific concentration is, in our view, the most parsimonious explanation for the outcome parity we observe across cosmetic-specific endpoints.

Second, ABPS / ACGME-PRS training remains superior on hospital-based complex reconstruction and free-flap microsurgery. This is not in dispute. Plastic surgery’s reconstructive scope — head-and-neck free flaps, complex breast reconstruction, lower-extremity salvage, congenital cleft — is the proper benchmark for those endpoints and the proper credentialing pathway for that work. The argument advanced here is narrower: for cosmetic outcomes, cosmetic-specific operative volume is the more relevant predictor than residency pathway. The AACS pathway concentrates that volume; the ABPS pathway distributes it across a broader curriculum.

Third, AACS fellows entering from general surgery bring complex-event management to the cosmetic OR. The case-log exemplar in Table 1 is representative of the operative scope general-surgery-trained AACS fellows bring with them: airway management at intubation, intra-operative pneumothorax recognition and decompression, complex intra-abdominal complications, vascular compromise, and damage-control surgery. These are the precise complications for which plastic-surgery-only-trained colleagues are sometimes consulted intra-operatively in cosmetic facilities; the general-surgery-trained cosmetic surgeon is — in many U.S. ambulatory facilities — the surgeon those calls are made to.

The clinical implication is not that one pathway is superior overall. The implication is that, for cosmetic procedures specifically, the appropriate comparison variable is cosmetic-specific operative volume and continuous outcomes data — not training-pathway badge. The LFPR provides the substrate to make that comparison ongoing, peer-reviewable, and publicly accountable.

Limitations

This analysis uses simulated values for outcome rates; the LFPR Phase 2 instrument was not yet at full enrollment at the time of mock submission. Comparator ABPS reference values are aggregated from published industry data sources that themselves carry response-bias limitations. Risk adjustment is bracketed rather than continuous for economic variables.

Conclusions

Within the cosmetic-procedure outcome envelope, AACS-fellowship-trained surgeons demonstrate clinical equipoise with ABPS-certified plastic surgeons. We argue cosmetic-specific operative volume — not residency-pathway badge — is the appropriate variable for predicting cosmetic-outcome quality. The LFPR provides the prospective substrate for ongoing comparison.

C  ·  THE PROJECTED 10-PAPER PIPELINE
Phase I → II → III scholarly roadmap

What the LFPR puts into AJCS — and the broader literature — in the next six years.

Sequenced low-risk to high-sensitivity. Every paper subject to Oversight Committee approval. No program-level comparison is published without program consent.

Phase I · Y1–Y2 · Low risk

01. National Fellowship Case Volume & Procedural Mix Benchmark

The 2026 update to Handler 2015 / Valina 2020. National medians, IQR, and distribution curves across the active AACS program network.

Phase I · Y1–Y2 · Low risk

02. Index Aesthetic Procedure Exposure Variability

Inter-program variability across 12 index procedures (rhinoplasty, facelift, breast, abdo, bleph, lipo, body contouring, injectables, lasers). Variance decomposition.

Phase I · Y1–Y2 · Low risk

03. Early Practice Transition Patterns After Fellowship

Practice structure, geographic distribution, and procedure-mix evolution at 1–3 years post-fellowship. The pipeline visibility piece.

Phase II · Y2–Y4 · Low–Mod

04. Time to Independent Operative Autonomy

Kaplan-Meier; Cox PH adjusting for specialty, practice model, geography. Months from fellowship completion to first independent aesthetic case.

Phase II · Y2–Y4 · Moderate

05. Fellowship Case Volume vs. Early Practice Productivity

Multivariable regression with COLI normalization and competition-density adjustment. Bracketed economic outcomes only.

Phase II · Y2–Y4 · Mod–High

06. Specialty Background & Fellowship Outcomes

Risk-adjusted stratified comparison across the multi-specialty entrants the Academy uniquely produces. Not published until n ≥ 100.

Phase III · Y3–Y6 · Moderate

07. Ownership Structure & Economic Trajectories

Solo / group / PE-backed / academic. COLI-adjusted, bracketed only. Committee review mandatory.

Phase III · Y3–Y6 · High

08. Predictors of High-Productivity Practices

Top-quartile volume/revenue model. Emphasizes structural predictors, not ranking. Committee revision authority essential.

Phase III · Y3–Y6 · Moderate

09. Evidence-Based Accreditation Volume Thresholds

ROC analysis identifying volume thresholds associated with acceptable complication rates and independence metrics. Moves AACS from opinion-based to data-based accreditation standards.

Phase III · Y4–Y6 · High

10. Return on Investment of Cosmetic Surgery Fellowship

NPV / DCF modeling with bracketed registry income data. Definitive recruitment artifact. Board-level review required.
